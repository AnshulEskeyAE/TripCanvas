# PHASE 3 — PROBLEM DISCOVERY

## 1. PURPOSE
The goal here was to take the 4 failure branches from Phase 2 out of the abstract and test them against real traveler behavior. I needed to see what planners actually do, and what makeshift tools they use to survive the booking process.

## 2. CORE QUESTION
What do lead organizers actually do when planning trips, and what workarounds have they invented to deal with booking friction?

## 3. INPUT
- Phase 2 Problem Space Map ([02 - Map the Problem Space.md](02%20-%20Map%20the%20Problem%20Space.md)) with the 4 candidate failure branches.
- Real interview responses from User 01 (Solo/Partner lead planner) and Group 02 (Peer group travelers).

---

## 4. WORK: USER RESEARCH & INTERVIEW LOGS

### Interview 1: Deep Dive with User 01 (Solo / Partner Lead Organizer)
* **Trip Context:** Solo travel and visiting partner in another city.
* **Q1: Booking Sequence:** Books flights first, then hotels. Why? Flights feel more price-sensitive and volatile. Hotel prices stay relatively stable, so the flight arrival time becomes the anchor for the hotel search.
* **Q2: Budget & Total Cost:** Often exceeded budget in the past due to checkout fees. **Coping Workaround:** Now mentally adds a buffer margin to every item to absorb unexpected taxes and fees.
* **Q3: Option Tracking Tool:** Uses a **Google Sheet with pros and cons** listed alongside prices to evaluate options side-by-side. Also starts with messy notes in Apple Notes first.
* **Q4: Social Feedback:** Takes second opinions from a partner before making major purchases, even when the partner is not paying or staying at the hotel, to reduce purchase anxiety.

---

### Interview 2: Peer Group Insights (3–5 Friends Planning Group Trips)
* **Q1: Booking Sequence (The "Dominant Expense" Rule):** 
  * They do not always book flights first. 
  * For short trips, they pick the accommodation first and choose cheaper road/train options.
  * For long-distance trips, flights dominate cost and safety, so flights are locked first.
  * *Takeaway:* Whichever cost dominates the trip budget gets locked in first; the secondary expense has to adapt.
* **Q2: Budget & Hidden Costs (The "Shock Absorber" Effect):** 
  * They try to estimate hidden fees early.
  * When booking costs exceed expectations, they do not cancel the trip—they cut back on activities, food, and shopping during the trip.
* **Q3: Discussions & Decision Speed:** 
  * Short weekend trips sometimes turn into "YOLO mode" (booking on the spot).
  * High-stakes or expensive trips involve long debates comparing stays versus travel trade-offs.
* **Q4: Group Authority (The "Dictator with Guardrails" Hack):**
  * Waiting for group chat consensus causes delays.
  * Successful organizers set upfront rules with the group (e.g., "under $60/night per person"), after which the organizer books everything autonomously without asking for approval on each item.

---

## 5. KEY BEHAVIORAL PATTERNS DISCOVERED

1. **The Dominant Expense Anchor:** People anchor their planning around the biggest single expense (flights for long distance, nice resort for short getaways) and let the other expenses absorb the trade-off.
2. **The In-Trip Shock Absorber:** Pre-booking price surprises directly steal budget from fun, food, and excursions during the trip.
3. **The Makeshift Comparison Matrix:** Organizers are forced into spreadsheets because no booking site lets them list pros, cons, and true total costs side by side.
4. **The Guardrail Delegation Pattern:** Organizers hate waiting for group chat replies, so they ask for upfront budget limits and then make decisions alone.

## 6. RESEARCH / ANALYSIS METHODS
- 1-on-1 retrospective user interviews.
- Artifact audit (inspecting notes apps and pros/cons spreadsheets).
- Qualitative synthesis across short-distance vs. long-distance trip behaviors.

## 7. OUTPUTS
- Qualitative interview records (User 01 + Peer Group).
- 4 verified user coping mechanisms (Buffer margins, pros/cons sheets, dominant expense anchoring, upfront guardrails).

## 8. DECISION
**GO (GATE 3 CLEARED)** — The problem hypotheses are backed by consistent, observable coping behaviors across both interviews (buffer margins, pros/cons sheets, dominant expense anchoring, upfront guardrails). This is qualitative evidence — not a prevalence study — but the behavioral patterns are clear enough to proceed.
